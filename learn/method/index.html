Method

How scoring works

What a mock exam score means, how the honest range is worked out, how the trap types were chosen, and what we deliberately do not claim.

The short version

  • Your score is the share of the questions you answered that you got right. Skipped questions are not counted against you.
  • A mock exam is only a sample, so we show the range your score supports (a 90% range) next to the score.
  • Where the exam guide says how much each topic counts, we add a range weighted that way.
  • Every question is tagged with one main trap type. Your Error Signature compares how often each type catches you.
  • None of this is a prediction that you will pass.

Your score

Accuracy is the questions you got right divided by the questions you answered. A question you skipped is not wrong: it is simply not answered, and it lowers how much evidence you have, not your accuracy. A question that asks for two answers is right only when you pick exactly the right two.

You can mark each answer Sure, Unsure or Guessing. That never changes your score. It sorts your answers into stable knowledge (right and sure), fragile knowledge (right but unsure), a likely misconception (wrong while sure) and a knowledge gap (wrong and unsure).

The pooled range

The first range treats the questions you answered as a sample of everything the exam could ask. It is the Wilson score interval, which behaves well for small samples and for scores near 0% or 100%, where the textbook “plus or minus” formula can run past the ends. We use a 90% level: if you sat many similar sets of questions at the same true level, about nine in ten ranges built this way would contain that level.

We round outward, the low end down and the high end up, so a range is never narrower than the maths says.

Worked example. 21 of 30 is 70%. The range is 55%–82%. The same 70% on twice as many questions (42 of 60) narrows to 59%–79%.

The formula

With k right out of n answered, p = k ÷ n and z = 1.6449 (the 95th percentile of the normal distribution, for a two-sided 90% range):

centre = (p + z² ÷ 2n) ÷ (1 + z² ÷ n)
half-width = z × √( p(1 − p) ÷ n + z² ÷ 4n² ) ÷ (1 + z² ÷ n)
range = centre ± half-width, kept between 0% and 100%

The weighted range

Not every topic counts the same on the real exam. When the vendor’s exam guide publishes a percentage for every topic, we also give a range weighted by those percentages. For an exam whose guide does not (or whose percentages do not add up to about 100%), the page says so and uses only the pooled range.

For each topic we estimate your true accuracy from what you answered there. A topic with no answers is treated as unknown, anything from 0% to 100%, instead of being dropped, so it widens the range rather than being ignored. We then combine the topics using the guide’s weights. Several uncertain topics added together have no tidy formula, so the combination is simulated: 20,000 draws, reporting the 5th to 95th percentile. The random numbers are seeded from your answers, so the same answers always give the same range, on any device.

Worked example. Four topics weighted 30%, 26%, 24%, 20% with results 6/8, 5/8, 5/7, 5/7 are still 70% pooled, but their weighted range is 54%–80%.

The details

For each topic with r right and w wrong answers we use a Beta(r + ½, w + ½) distribution (the Jeffreys prior, which assumes nothing about you). Each draw samples every topic, then adds them up as score = Σ weight × accuracy, with the weights scaled to sum to 1. Gamma samples come from the Marsaglia and Tsang method, normal samples from Box and Muller, and the generator is Mulberry32 seeded by hashing the right, answered and weight figures of every topic. Percentiles are linearly interpolated and rounded outward.

Comparing two attempts

When you retake a mock exam, your result says whether the change is bigger than the noise. If the two pooled ranges do not overlap, the change is called real. If they overlap, the difference may just be which questions you happened to get. This is deliberately cautious: it can miss a small real improvement, but it will not invent one.

Trap types and your Error Signature

Every mock exam question comes with an Exam Trap note explaining how its wrong answers mislead. For the 1,310 questions in these 43 mock exams, a CertShield editor read each note and gave the question one main trap type out of nine (see the Trap Atlas). A note can describe more than one trap, so the tag records the main one. It is a judgement, not a measurement.

Each tag is stored with a fingerprint of the note’s text. If a question is rewritten, its tag is set aside and the question shows as unclassified until it is read again, so a stale tag never survives an edit.

Your Error Signature. After a mock exam we count, for each trap type, how many of its questions you answered and how many you missed. A type is flagged when you answered at least 4 of its questions, missed at least 2, and missed at least 20 percentage points more often than your overall miss rate. We show up to three. With so few questions this is a pointer to look at, not a diagnosis.

What we do not claim

  • A chance of passing. Many exams report a scaled score, or only pass or fail, and a practice percentage cannot be converted into either. Two of the exams here publish a percentage pass mark, but a practice test and the real exam differ in difficulty, so we still do not turn the range into a verdict.
  • That the range covers everything. It reflects the luck of which questions you got. It cannot see whether a mock exam is easier or harder than the real one.
  • That a mock exam is the real exam. These are original questions. We do not have, use or publish real exam content.
  • Weights we cannot source. Weights are the vendor’s own published percentages, some of them approximate or averages (marked ~). Where there are none, none are used.
  • That the trap tags are objective. They are one editor’s reading of each question.

How we check it

The calculations run in your browser. The same method is written a second time in Python, and the two are tested against each other on a set of cases, including every worked example on this page: they must give the same answers. Exam facts and topic weights are read from each mock exam’s own source data, not retyped by hand, and an exam whose weights cannot be matched to its topics is shown without them.
